<h2>TDS vs hardness (TDS is not hardness)</h2>
<p>Many homes ask if a high TDS number means hard water. Not always. This is one of the most common mistakes when people search how to test hard water at home.</p>
<p><strong>TDS</strong> (total dissolved solids) is a broad count of dissolved material. It can include hardness minerals, plus other salts and ions.</p>
<p><strong>Hardness</strong> is mainly calcium and magnesium that form scale when heated.</p>
<ul>
<li>High TDS can happen with hard water, but high TDS is not proof of hardness by itself.</li>
<li>A lower TDS reading does not always mean “no scale”.</li>
<li>A boil-kettle white film is closer to a hardness / scale check than a single TDS number.</li>
</ul>
<p>If you use a TDS pen, treat it as supporting context only. Always pair it with the boiling test for hard water. If you need hardness in ppm or mg/L as CaCO₃, ask a lab.</p>

<h2>FAQ: how to test hard water at home</h2>

<h3>How do I test hard water at home without a kit?</h3>
<p>Use the boil-kettle test: boil tank-fed tap water in a clean vessel, pour it out, and check for white crust. Also check the cooled top layer for floating white particles, then look at taps and the geyser.</p>

<h3>How to check hard water in 5 minutes?</h3>
<p>Clean vessel, fill from the overhead-tank tap, boil 5 to 10 minutes, pour out, inspect for white film. That boiling test is the fastest useful screen.</p>

<h3>Is the boiling test for hard water accurate?</h3>
<p>It is a practical screen for scale-forming minerals, not a lab hardness number. Thick white film after boiling is a strong signal. No film does not always mean soft water, so still check bathroom scale.</p>

<h3>Can I use a TDS meter to check hard water?</h3>
<p>A TDS meter measures total dissolved solids, not hardness alone. Use it as backup. Prefer the boil test and fixture scale for home scale risk.</p>

<h3>What is the difference between TDS and hardness?</h3>
<p>Hardness is mainly calcium and magnesium that form scale. TDS counts many dissolved substances. High TDS can include hardness, but the two are not the same test.</p>

<h3>Does clear water mean soft water?</h3>
<p>No. Hard water can look clear in a glass and still leave white stains after heating or drying.</p>

<h3>Should I test bathroom water or RO water?</h3>
<p>For whole-house scale, test bathroom or kitchen water that comes from the overhead tank. RO water only tells you about the drinking line.</p>

<h3>Why did my hard water test at home change after a tanker?</h3>
<p>Tanker and borewell water vary. Retest after each major source change. Size treatment for your harder usual pattern.</p>

<h3>What should I do after a positive hard water test at home?</h3>
<p>If you own the overhead tank, size a salt-free tank unit to your litres and daily use. Start with Mini, Semi, or Centi on the collection page. Keep RO for drinking if you already use it.</p>

<h3>Will Scale-O change soap lather?</h3>
<p>No. Scale-O is salt-free chelation for scale control. Soap lather usually stays the same. Better lather typically needs salt-based ion exchange that removes hardness minerals.</p>

<h3>Will a water softener change my TDS reading a lot?</h3>
<p>A salt-free chelation unit conditions minerals so they are less likely to form hard scale. Do not expect the same TDS or strip change as a salt-based ion-exchange plant.</p>

<h3>Does a positive test mean I must buy a salt plant?</h3>
<p>Not always. Many Indian houses with overhead tanks choose a salt-free tank insert to reduce scale without salt, drain, and electricity. Read the salt-free vs salt-based guide before you choose.</p>

<h3>Is Scale-O for apartments?</h3>
<p>Only if you control an overhead or loft tank the unit can sit in. Typical apartment shaft lines without a private tank are not the product use case.</p>

<h3>How often should I repeat a hard water test at home?</h3>
<p>Retest when your source changes (new borewell season, heavy tanker use) or when scale suddenly increases. Otherwise a careful test every few months is enough for most homes.</p>
